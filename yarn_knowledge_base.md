# The Yarn Nest — Editorial Knowledge Base

Purpose: A growing reference for the article generator. This file is starter knowledge, not a substitute for checking current product specifications, manufacturer instructions, pattern details, or authoritative craft references.

## Core craft terminology

- Yarn: A continuous strand of fibers used in crafts such as knitting, crochet, weaving, and some forms of macrame.
- Crochet: A fabric-making technique that uses a crochet hook to draw loops through other loops. Crochet stitches and construction methods differ from knitting.
- Knitting: A fabric-making technique that forms and works with loops on knitting needles or other knitting tools. Do not use knitting instructions as crochet instructions.
- Weaving: A technique that interlaces yarn or other strands, commonly crossing lengthwise and crosswise elements.
- Macrame: A knotting-based fiber craft. It is not the same as crochet or knitting.
- Amigurumi: The crochet or knitting craft of making small stuffed toys or figures. Many amigurumi patterns are crocheted, but the term itself should not be treated as a stitch or yarn type.
- Gauge: The number of stitches and rows measured over a specified area, usually stated in a pattern. It affects finished size and yarn use. Follow the individual pattern's measurement method.
- Yarn weight: A classification describing yarn thickness, such as lace, fingering, sport, DK, worsted, bulky, or super bulky. Naming conventions and actual thickness can vary; check the yarn label and pattern.
- Fiber content: The material a yarn is made from, such as cotton, wool, acrylic, alpaca, or a blend. Do not assume all yarns in one broad category have identical properties.
- Skein, ball, hank, and cake: Common forms in which yarn is packaged. They are not necessarily interchangeable terms for the same winding format.

## Common yarn families and careful descriptions

- Cotton yarn: A plant-fiber yarn commonly used for items where stitch definition and care instructions matter. Do not claim it is always best for a project; suitability depends on the pattern, construction, and label.
- Wool yarn: Animal-fiber yarn. Properties vary by breed, processing, and blend. Do not claim every wool yarn is machine washable or suitable for people with sensitivities.
- Acrylic yarn: A synthetic fiber yarn available in many weights and finishes. Care and heat tolerance vary by product; follow the label.
- Mohair yarn: Yarn made from the hair of the Angora goat, often used for a light, fuzzy halo. Avoid blanket claims about softness or suitability for sensitive skin.
- Alpaca yarn: Fiber from alpacas. Different preparations and blends behave differently; check the product label.
- Chenille yarn: A yarn with a plush, velvety surface. Stitch visibility and handling can differ from smoother yarns; advise checking the specific pattern and yarn.
- T-shirt yarn: A usually thick yarn-like material made from strips or tubes of T-shirt fabric, often used for baskets, rugs, and accessories. Thickness and stretch vary.
- Velvet yarn: A plush-textured yarn. It may obscure stitch definition, so tutorials should explain how to identify stitches carefully when relevant.
- Worsted weight: A common medium yarn-weight category in US patterns. Never assume a particular hook size or gauge without checking the pattern and yarn label.
- DK (double knitting): A common yarn-weight term. Do not confuse it with a guarantee of a specific gauge or needle/hook size.

## Article-writing accuracy rules

1. Never invent a pattern, stitch count, hook size, needle size, gauge, yardage, finished dimensions, wash temperature, or fiber property.
2. When a project requires exact specifications, either cite a reliable pattern/manufacturer source in the article workflow or clearly tell readers to follow the yarn label and the chosen pattern.
3. Distinguish beginner-friendly advice from universal rules. Explain when a recommendation depends on the project.
4. For project tutorials, state the skill level, materials, approximate time only if grounded, and any assumptions. If exact steps cannot be reliably supplied, write a buying/selection/overview guide instead of pretending to provide a tested pattern.
5. For brand or product articles, verify current product names, fiber content, weights, yardage, colors, availability, and care details against the manufacturer's current information before making specific claims.
6. Do not present user reviews, product tests, personal crafting experience, or quotes unless those materials were actually supplied and verified.
7. Do not use competitor article text as a template. Build an original explanation around the reader's question.

## Knowledge growth process

For every new topic, add verified notes here after checking trustworthy sources such as yarn manufacturers' product labels and care pages, recognized craft education references, and the original pattern designer's instructions. Record the source name, URL, date checked, and the exact claim supported. Prefer primary sources for product specifications. If sources disagree, describe the variation instead of inventing certainty.

## Reader-first standards

- Answer the reader's main question early.
- Use plain US English and explain craft vocabulary.
- Make instructions scannable and specific.
- Include troubleshooting only when it is relevant to the project.
- Use FAQs to answer genuine follow-up questions, not to repeat keywords.
- Avoid filler, keyword stuffing, fabricated experience, and guaranteed SEO claims.
